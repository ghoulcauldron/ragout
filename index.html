Ragoût

ragoût ra-GOO
A stew cooked slowly until the toughest cuts turn tender.
ragout RAG-out
Laundry-plant word for condemned linen headed to the grinder.

We rescue the second kind and treat it like the first: slow-cooked workwear from New York’s discarded kitchen aprons.

Scroll to cook it. Press and hold the apron to crumple it.

1

Rescue

Commercial linen plants in Long Island City, Maspeth and the Bronx condemn poly-cotton bib aprons for baked-on grease and small burns. We buy them by the pound before they’re baled into wipers.

2

Scour

One hot wash with Synthrapol and soda ash strips the grease. It’s the only step that spends heat, and the runoff goes through a Filtrol 160 microfiber filter before it reaches a drain.

3

Crumple

Low water immersion. Each apron is packed tight into a small container instead of a 30:1 vat, using up to 80% less water. The folds become resists.

4

Dye

Fiber-reactive dye bonds to the 35% cotton at room temperature, with no heating. The polyester refuses it, and that refusal is the heathered, mottled surface.

5

Unfold

Every crease leaves a record. No two aprons come out of the bath alike.

6

Mend

Burn holes get a patch and heavy cotton sashiko. Melted polyester ties are replaced with cotton twill tape. The damage stays visible.

Synthetic dye, natural names

Each colorway is a room-temperature fiber-reactive mix tuned to sit near a natural dye’s color, without the boiling water and metal mordants natural dyeing needs. Tap one to re-dye the apron.

Wash it less

A white apron shows every smear, so it goes in the wash every shift. Mottled, over-dyed cloth takes the day’s stains into its patina. Fewer washes means fewer polyester microfibers shed into the water over the apron’s second life.

Nothing basic goes down the drain

Fiber-reactive dye needs soda ash, which leaves the bath around pH 10.5 to 11. Before disposal, every bath is brought back to 6.5–8.5 with food-grade citric acid. Try it.
